Which Ski Resorts Have the Best Lift Redundancy? An N-1 Test on 1,888 Lift Networks in 52 Countries

October 8, 2026 · 11 min read

Close one lift, measure what terrain you can no longer lap. Les 3 Vallées loses at most 5%. Panorama loses 64% when Champagne Express stops.

A chair goes on wind hold, or a gearbox fails on a Tuesday. How much of the mountain still works? We ran that test on every lift network we could build from OpenStreetMap, 1,967 ski areas in 54 countries: close one lift, see what terrain you can no longer lap, put it back, close the next one. Power-grid engineers call this an N-1 contingency check. Of the 435 resorts with at least ten lifts in their main loop and a well-connected map, the median worst single closure takes out 12.9% of the lappable piste. Big Alpine domains like Les 3 Vallées (134 loop lifts, 5.2%) barely notice any one lift. At Panorama in British Columbia, closing Champagne Express cuts off 64%.

Step 1. Turn the mountain into a graph

The inputs are the public lifts.parquet and pistes.parquet files from Download Data. Every lift becomes two stations, bottom and top, joined by a one-way ride. Gondolas, cable cars, and funiculars get a ride down as well, because you can usually download them. Every downhill piste (plus piste:type=connection links) becomes a chain of one-way segments, pointing the way the OSM way is drawn. OSM convention is that pistes are drawn downhill and lifts bottom to top.

Pistes join where their vertices sit within 30 m of each other. A station connects to any piste vertex within 100 m, which covers the walk from the unload ramp to the first turn. That's the whole graph: 18,181 lifts across 1,967 ski areas that have at least three lifts and some mapped downhill runs.

OSM isn't perfect about direction. Mount Sunapee's summit express, for example, is drawn from the summit down. So before building the graph, the script lets nearby pistes vote: runs should start near a lift's top and end near its bottom. If the vote clearly disagrees with the drawn direction, the lift is flipped. 531 of the 18,181 lifts got flipped that way. The vote isn't certain: Sunapee's express didn't get a clear enough margin to flip, so Sunapee isn't quoted anywhere below.

Mount Sunapee. Orange lines are lifts, colored lines are pistes, and the graph is drawn from exactly these objects.

Step 2. Define "still works"

A loop is a set of stations you can circulate between forever: ski down, ride up, repeat. In graph terms it's a strongly connected component. A piste segment is lappable if you can ski onto it from a loop and ski off it back into the same loop. We measure terrain in kilometers of lappable piste, not trail count, because one long run split into six OSM ways shouldn't count six times.

For each lift in the main loop, the script removes the ride and recomputes every loop. Two different things can happen.

Terrain lost

Segments that were lappable before and aren't lappable from any loop now. This is the "that pod is closed" number, and it's what the rankings below use.

Resort split

Segments that are still lappable, but only from a loop that's no longer connected to the main one. Close the hinge lift and the mountain becomes two smaller resorts. You can still ski the far side if you're already there, but you can't get there from the base.

Each resort gets a worst single closure (the max terrain lost), a mean closure across all its loop lifts, and a worst split. A resort is scored only if at least 60% of its mapped piste sits inside some loop, which leaves 1,888 of the 1,967 areas.

Most resorts with 10+ lifts have one lift worth 10–20% of their terrain.

Vail. Closing Tea Cup Express loses only 2.2% of the terrain but splits 20.6% of the network (Blue Sky Basin and China Bowl) away from the front side.

Step 3. More lifts, more redundancy

Size is the first-order effect. A five-lift hill usually has one lift that is the mountain. By twenty lifts, the network has enough overlapping pods that a single failure is a nuisance rather than a closure. These medians are across every scored area:

The mean closure falls much faster than the worst. Big resorts are full of lifts that duplicate each other, but almost every one still has a single lift guarding a pod with nothing else on it. That lift sets the worst-case number. Even the 27 domains with 40+ loop lifts, almost all of them in the Alps, still lose a median 6.3% to their worst closure.

Step 7. Splits: when one mountain becomes two

Arosa Lenzerheide is the cleanest split case in the dataset. The two resorts were joined in 2014 by the Urdenbahn cable car across the Urdental. Close it and 89% of the network detaches from the other half. Both sides keep running, so terrain lost is small (5.7%), but anyone who crossed in the morning is on the wrong side of a valley.

Park City does the same along the old Canyons-Park City boundary. Close Iron Mountain Express and 42% of the network splits off. KitzSki's 3S Bahn (41.6%) and Ski Arlberg's Trittkopfbahn I (41.9%) are the Austrian versions: one long cable link between ski areas that used to be separate. Vail's Tea Cup Express (20.6%) is the North American classic, the hinge between the front side and the back bowls.

Splits matter more for guests than lost terrain does. A closed pod is a shorter trail list. A split is a skier who rode over in the morning and can't get back to their car.

Some split numbers are the map talking, not the mountain. Dolomiti Superski, Hochoetz-Kühtai, and Champéry-Les Crosets show 100% splits because one OSM area outlines several resorts the piste network doesn't actually join. They're left out of the split chart.

Link lifts between formerly separate resorts are the classic split.

Park City. Two lifts form the hinge between the Canyons side (west) and the original Park City side (east).

Step 8. What this model can't see

This is a topology test on open map data, so read the numbers as "how the network is shaped," not "what ops will open tomorrow."

How resorts are drawn

A resort is whatever one OSM ski-area outline covers. Umbrella areas like Dolomiti Superski or Paradiski group resorts the pistes don't connect. Cross-border domains are split by country, so Zermatt and Breuil-Cervinia score as two halves. Lifts more than 2 km from any outline aren't assigned to a resort at all.

No elevation

Direction comes from how ways are drawn plus the flip vote. A piste drawn uphill breaks a path. A traverse drawn the wrong way can invent one. A DEM lookup would settle direction properly.

Correlated failures

N-1 assumes one lift fails at a time. Real wind holds close every exposed summit lift at once, which is when redundancy matters most. Lift capacity and lift lines aren't modeled either: losing one of two parallel six-packs is 0% terrain lost and still a miserable morning.

Walking and roads

Anything more than 100 m from a station doesn't count as connected. A village walk, a skier bridge, or a ski bus can join two loops that this model treats as separate. That matters most in the Alps, where linked villages often rely on exactly those.

The fix for most of these is upstream. Draw lifts bottom to top, draw pistes downhill, and tag catwalks and skier bridges as piste:type=connection. The tagging walkthrough covers the basics, and the lift type guide covers which aerialway values count as uphill transport.

Big White. Gem Lake Express is the only way out of 35% of the lappable terrain.

Takeaway

The resorts with the best lift redundancy are big, multi-valley networks with overlapping pods. Les 3 Vallées, Serre Chevalier, Saalbach, Tignes-Val d'Isère, Vail, and Whistler Blackcomb each stay above 90% open whichever lift you close. Compact Nordic hills like Trysil do even better on a percentage basis, because their lifts overlap on one face. The most fragile networks put a whole drainage behind one chair, and western Canada has the most of them. In the Alps, the weak point is more often a split: the one gondola that links two valleys. If your favorite runs are behind either kind of lift, check its status before you drive.

The script is scripts/lift-redundancy.mjs in the site repo. It reads the same parquet files you can download, runs in under a minute once the files are cached, and has a --self-test with a three-chair toy mountain. For per-lift details on the atlas side, start with Ski Lift Facts.
