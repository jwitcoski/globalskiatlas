What Ski Resort Stats Actually Mean

September 1, 2026 · 6 min read

Vertical drop, skiable acres, trail counts, and lift totals — and why mapped OpenStreetMap data often differs from resort marketing.

Every resort website repeats vertical drop, skiable acres, trail count, and lift totals until those numbers feel objective, yet they are often defined differently from one mountain to the next. Global Ski Atlas publishes statistics derived from the same OpenStreetMap geometry for every downhill area we index, so you can compare a Colorado mom-and-pop hill to a Tyrolean valley on equal footing. This essay argues that mapped open data—not brochure copy—should drive fair comparison, and it explains what our core stats mean, why they diverge from marketing, and how to use resort comparison once you understand the columns.

Vertical drop and skiable terrain are the two stats resorts advertise most aggressively and map most inconsistently. We compute vertical relief from elevation models and tagged features inside the resort boundary, reflecting skiable high and low points rather than a single sign at the lodge. Marketing often claims summit-to-base figures you cannot ski in one continuous run, while we may include sub-peaks inside the polygon if they are mapped. Skiable acres in our dataset come from the area of mapped piste:type=downhill geometry inside the boundary, not from proprietary acreage formulas that double-count ridge sides or hike-to bowls. When brochure acreage dwarfs mapped totals, the gap usually means missing OSM lines or an oversized landuse=winter_sports polygon, not a bug in our math; see largest ski resorts in the world for how mapped size rankings behave.

Trail count and lift count describe capacity and variety but depend on how thoroughly mappers have drawn the mountain. We count distinct OSM ways tagged piste:type=downhill after deduplication, so one logical run split into upper and lower segments may appear as two trails, while unmapped gladed runs contribute nothing. Lifts are mapped aerialway=* features in the resort window, grouped by type on Ski Lift Facts; rope tows and magic carpets are often under-tagged, and pylons mistakenly tagged as lifts can distort totals until corrected per ski lift types explained. Trail mix—the percentage of green, blue, and black tagged runs—powers beginner scoring in best ski resorts for beginners, but US and European difficulty tags are not interchangeable, and untagged runs land in an unknown bucket.

When atlas numbers look wrong, the fix is usually upstream in OpenStreetMap rather than in our comparison table. Missing piste:type=downhill or aerialway tags, bloated boundaries, and pipeline lag behind live edits are the usual causes; previewing terrain on the interactive map or in the ski game quickly shows whether runs are absent from data. Researchers can inspect attributes in GeoParquet from Download Data, and contributors can follow how to tag a ski resort in OpenStreetMap to improve the next build. Stats narrow a shortlist; they do not replace snow reports, lessons, or village atmosphere covered in how to choose a ski resort.

Ski resort statistics are only as honest as their definitions, and we choose mapped OpenStreetMap geometry so those definitions stay constant across countries and seasons. Learn vertical, acres, trails, lifts, and trail mix as we compute them, compare candidates in resort comparison, and when brochures and maps disagree, trust the reproducible source—then fix OSM so the next pipeline run tells a truer story for everyone using Global Ski Atlas.
