Magazine lists of the world’s largest ski resorts rarely agree because each repeats resort marketing that measures “size” differently—acres, kilometers, interconnected domains, or ticket boundaries. Global Ski Atlas ranks downhill areas by mapped trail length: the sum of tagged `piste` ways inside each resort boundary from OpenStreetMap, analyzed the same way on every continent. This essay argues that mapped kilometers provide a fairer yardstick than brochure copy, names the resort domains that consistently dominate that measure, and shows how to verify live rankings on the [interactive map](../mainmap.html) and in [resort comparison](../resort-comparison.html).

Our methodology clips downhill `piste` lines inside OSM resort polygons and sums their lengths, with lift counts and difficulty mixes as supporting context explained in [ski resort stats explained](ski-resort-stats-explained.html). The approach captures interconnected European mega-domains and large North American consolidations when mappers have traced pistes thoroughly, but it undercounts unmapped off-piste and can mis-rank areas where volunteers have not yet drawn every connector. Marketing acreage often includes hike-to terrain or loosely affiliated villages; mapped kilometers reward what is on the map today, which is why a French area may look smaller than its brochure until a local mapper spends a week tracing Val Thorens, and why two similarly sized mountains can rank differently based on tagging culture alone.

Several names reliably appear at the top of live data sorted by mapped trail length, even as exact order shifts with each OSM edit. Les Trois Vallées and Portes du Soleil in France and Switzerland represent the densest European networks, with Matterhorn Ski Paradise, Paradiski, Espace Killy, and Dolomiti-linked domains close behind. In North America, consolidated Utah and Tahoe footprints and growing Japanese coverage at Niseko United illustrate how merger and mapping enthusiasm inflate mapped size independently of skier perception. Whistler, Grandvalira, and the Jungfrau region often sit just outside the top tier depending on boundary tagging. These are guided landmarks, not a frozen medal table—always check the current dataset before quoting a rank.

Disagreement between marketing and mapped size is a feature of open data, not a failure of it. Resorts have every incentive to maximize headline figures; volunteers have every incentive to trace what exists on the ground. Global Ski Atlas publishes what is drawn today so skiers can compare Les Trois Vallées to Zermatt on one scale, preview terrain on the [map](../mainmap.html), and settle bets in [resort comparison](../resort-comparison.html). The experimental [ski game](/playable/) loads the same geometry for a playful sense of sprawl versus a compact eastern hill, and [trail facts](../SkiTrailFacts.html) helps identify the longest individual runs inside a domain. When a brochure claims ten new kilometers, check whether those lines exist in OSM yet—someone still has to draw them.

The world’s largest ski resorts are mostly the names you already know; open data changes how you measure them. Trust mapped trail kilometers from the live atlas for rankings, use marketing for photos and vibe, and remember that completeness varies by region as discussed in [how many ski resorts worldwide](how-many-ski-resorts-worldwide.html). Mapped size is honest, reproducible, and improvable—exactly the standard a worldwide downhill atlas should apply.
