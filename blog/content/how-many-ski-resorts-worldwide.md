Ask how many ski resorts exist worldwide and you will hear wildly different answers because every list uses different rules. Global Ski Atlas answers the question with one repeatable method: we count mapped downhill ski areas from OpenStreetMap that meet consistent criteria, analyze them with the same pipeline everywhere, and publish the results on the [interactive map](../mainmap.html) and in [Ski Resort Facts](../SkiResortFacts.html). This essay states our thesis—that an honest global count comes from open geometry, not marketing—and explains our methodology, regional patterns, and known gaps.

We start each entry from a `landuse=winter_sports` polygon and extract nearby `piste:type=downhill` ways and `aerialway=*` lifts within roughly two kilometers of that boundary. A resort is included when it has a identifiable winter-sports geometry and at least one mapped downhill piste or lift; we do not require minimum vertical or acreage, so small community hills count alongside destination mountains if they are tagged coherently. The full pipeline—from Geofabrik extracts through analysis to GeoParquet—is documented on [Download Data](../DownloadData.html). That reproducibility is the point: you can download the files and verify our totals instead of accepting a magazine’s rounded figure.

Continental totals reflect both real geography and mapping culture, not bragging rights. Europe, especially the Alps, contributes hundreds of well-mapped areas; North America combines fewer polygons with larger average size, including a long US tail of Midwest and Northeast hills detailed in [which U.S. states have the most ski resorts](us-states-most-ski-resorts.html). Asia’s count has grown as Japan and other regions gained mapper attention; Oceania, Africa, and South America add smaller but meaningful footprints where each new tag shifts the ranking. As of recent combined builds, the atlas indexes on the order of three thousand to four thousand distinct downhill areas worldwide, with the exact number moving as OSM editors add hills or clean duplicates. Les Trois Vallées may appear as one polygon or several depending on local relations, so we report resort count rather than lift-ticket marketing domains.

OpenStreetMap coverage is uneven, and our count is only as good as the map beneath it. Under-mapping—boundaries without lifts or pistes inside—excludes areas until someone follows [how to tag a ski resort in OpenStreetMap](how-to-tag-a-ski-resort-in-openstreetmap.html). Over-mapping, such as hiking paths tagged as pistes or oversized polygons, inflates stats until boundaries are corrected. We exclude nordic-only areas without downhill terrain, most indoor domes, heli-only operations without mapped in-bounds runs, and placeholders that lack both lifts and pistes. Commercial directories that report only “significant” destinations often land near two thousand resorts because they apply revenue or vertical thresholds we deliberately avoid.

The number of downhill ski resorts in the world is therefore both a geographic fact and a snapshot of community mapping progress. Global Ski Atlas does not sell passes or endorse destinations; we publish comparable open data so skiers can explore on the [map](../mainmap.html), compare in [resort comparison](../resort-comparison.html), and improve the count where they ski. Check live aggregates on [Ski Resort Facts](../SkiResortFacts.html), and when your local hill is missing, tag it—the global total changes one coherent resort at a time.
