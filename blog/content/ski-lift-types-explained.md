Lift technology shapes your ski day as much as trail color: ten high-speed quads make a resort feel smaller than three slow doubles serving the same acreage, and beginners panic on T-bars while families prefer gondolas in wind. Global Ski Atlas catalogs every mapped `aerialway` in OpenStreetMap and rolls counts per resort and country on [Ski Lift Facts](../SkiLiftFacts.html). This essay explains why lift type matters for resort choice, how OSM tags map to skier language, and which tagging mistakes skew the data skiers rely on when comparing mountains.

OSM uses `aerialway=*` on ways following cable paths: `chair_lift` and `detachable` for chairs, `gondola` and `cable_car` for enclosed carriers, `t-bar`, `platter`, `rope_tow`, and `magic_carpet` for surface lifts, with `mixed_lift` for chondolas. Fixed-grip chairs cap throughput on busy weekends; detachables shrink a mountain psychologically by cutting ride time; gondolas protect beginners and link valleys in the Alps. Worldwide, chairs dominate North America while gondolas cluster in Europe and Japan and drags remain common in Austria and Scandinavia—patterns visible when sorting [lift facts](../SkiLiftFacts.html) by country and pairing with [ski resort stats explained](ski-resort-stats-explained.html) for resort-level totals.

Tagging errors propagate into comparison tables until mappers fix them, which is why lift literacy matters for data quality as well as trip planning. The most frequent mistake maps lift pylons as lifts or duplicates parallel ways after remapping, inflating counts or creating phantom capacity. Wrong subtypes—carpets tagged as rope tows, detachable quads as fixed chairs—mislead beginner research tied to [best ski resorts for beginners](best-ski-resorts-for-beginners.html). Lifts drawn outside resort polygons do not count toward that resort even if skiers use them daily, so boundary fixes often matter more than retagging lines, as covered in [how to tag a ski resort in OpenStreetMap](how-to-tag-a-ski-resort-in-openstreetmap.html).

Match lifts to your group when choosing where to ski. First-day beginners should prioritize magic carpets and short fixed chairs; families benefit from gondolas; powder hounds want detachable quads for lap count; budget road trippers often find cheaper tickets at drag-lift hills in high-count states from [which U.S. states have the most ski resorts](us-states-most-ski-resorts.html). Explore mixes on the [interactive map](../mainmap.html), compare Beaver Creek to a drag-heavy Austrian hill in [resort comparison](../resort-comparison.html), and preview layout in the [ski game](/playable/). [How to choose a ski resort](how-to-choose-a-ski-resort.html) folds lift mix into a broader framework alongside trail maps from [how to read a ski trail map](how-to-read-ski-trail-map.html).

Lifts are the plumbing of a ski area; OSM’s `aerialway` vocabulary works when mappers use it consistently. Learn the types, spot tagging errors, check [lift facts](../SkiLiftFacts.html) before a six-hour drive to the wrong hill, and edit OSM when you find mistakes—the atlas refreshes from community data, and accurate lift tags make fair comparison possible across every downhill area we index.
