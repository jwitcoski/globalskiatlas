Epic Pass vs Ikon Pass: What Our Database Actually Covers

September 1, 2026 · 6 min read

How many atlas resorts each pass includes, where they overlap, and what thousands of independent hills are left out.

Epic Pass and Ikon Pass dominate North American ski marketing, yet their resort lists change every spring and represent a small slice of the downhill areas Global Ski Atlas indexes from OpenStreetMap. This essay states that pass “coverage” must be measured against an open global database—not logo counts—and explains how we match partners to atlas resorts, where Epic and Ikon footprints concentrate, and why thousands of independents remain outside both rosters. Verify all access rules on official pass sites for the 2026–27 season before purchasing; our figures describe atlas alignment at publication time, not legal entitlements.

Pass roster coverage and atlas database coverage answer different questions. Epic centers on Vail Resorts-owned mountains plus selected partners with tiered day limits and blackouts; Ikon centers on Alterra destinations plus a broader coalition including Utah icons, Jackson Hole, Aspen, and select international partners with similar restrictions. Our atlas maps roughly three thousand downhill areas worldwide—including municipals, rope-tow hills, and independents—with on the order of nine hundred in North America per the regional split on the book pitch page. When we say a pass “covers” an atlas resort, we fuzzy-match a named partner to a downhill area by name and location; false positives and negatives occur, and a fully mapped independent may offer better terrain per dollar than a pass icon with blackout days, comparably shown in resort comparison with columns defined in ski resort stats explained.

Epic skews toward Colorado, Utah, Tahoe, the Northeast US, and flagship international partners such as Whistler, with Midwest and Mid-Atlantic areas often smaller in mapped trail count but valuable for local laps. Ikon competes through Alta, Snowbird, Brighton, Solitude, Aspen Snowmass, Jackson Hole, Big Sky, Winter Park, and a wider Northeast and Canada footprint useful for loops in multi-resort road trip planner, plus limited European and Japanese partners with strict day caps. Overlap concentrates in Colorado, Utah, and the Northeast; buying both passes rarely clears break-even except for split households, so most skiers optimize one network plus indie days as argued in Epic vs Ikon vs Indy vs Europe. Plot partners on the interactive map and count unaffiliated dots within drive time rings to see what pass marketing omits—especially in states leading which U.S. states have the most ski resorts.

Indy Pass and day tickets serve the long tail Epic and Ikon under-represent: hundreds of North American downhill areas outside both rosters, many modest in trail count but strong for learning terrain per best ski resorts for beginners. Europe remains a continent of roughly nine hundred atlas areas where lift tickets and village logistics dominate; overseas partner days on US passes are supplements, not a continent-wide strategy. Researchers can join partner lists to atlas IDs using Parquet from Download Data; recreational planners should use trip planner, weather map, and tier ranking after verifying rosters at epicpass.com and ikonpass.com.

Epic and Ikon cover prominent slices of the ski world, not the ski world itself. Atlas data shows where partners cluster, where overlap wastes money, and where independents hide on the map—then how to choose a ski resort and the decision framework in Epic vs Ikon vs Indy vs Europe help you pick a strategy. Compare terrain with our tools, trust mapped stats over slogans, and remember that improving OSM via how to tag a ski resort in OpenStreetMap makes pass-versus-indie comparisons truer for the next season.
